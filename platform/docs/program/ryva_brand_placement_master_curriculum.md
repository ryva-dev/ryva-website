# 1. Program Design Philosophy

The Ryva Program should not feel like a collection of generic videos followed by trivia questions.

The learning model is:

> **Concept → Context → Decision → Practice → Reflection**

Every major concept should answer four questions:

1. **What is this?**
2. **Why does it matter in brand placement?**
3. **What information would someone pay attention to?**
4. **How does this connect to the larger commercial system?**

The objective is to help learners understand common industry structures, terminology, calculations, documents, relationships, and commercial considerations through independent education and guided practice.

The Program should not present itself as occupational certification, professional qualification, employment preparation, job placement, or an earnings program.

# 5. Commercial Math Reference

Math should appear naturally throughout the Program.

Learners should be able to calculate and interpret the following.

## Order value

**Units × wholesale price = line value**

Example:

18 units × $42 wholesale = **$756**

For a multi-SKU order, add the extended value of every line.

## Retail markup

**Markup % = (Retail Price − Wholesale Price) ÷ Wholesale Price × 100**

Example:

Wholesale = $40  
Retail = $80

Markup = ($80 − $40) ÷ $40 = **100%**

## Retail gross margin

**Gross Margin % = (Retail Price − Wholesale Price) ÷ Retail Price × 100**

Example:

Wholesale = $40  
Retail = $80

Gross margin = ($80 − $40) ÷ $80 = **50%**

### Critical teaching point

Markup and margin are not the same calculation.

A product with a 100% markup from $40 to $80 produces a 50% gross margin for the retailer before other expenses.

## Gross profit dollars

**Retail Price − Wholesale Price = Gross Profit Dollars per unit**

Example:

$80 − $40 = **$40 gross profit dollars per unit**

## Discounted order value

**Order Value × (1 − Discount %) = Discounted Order Value**

Example:

$6,000 order with 10% discount:

$6,000 × 0.90 = **$5,400**

## Commission

**Commissionable Sales × Commission Rate = Expected Commission**

Example:

$7,560 × 12% = **$907.20**

## Adjusted commission

If $500 of an order becomes non-commissionable:

Original commissionable value: $7,560  
Adjusted value: $7,060  
Rate: 12%

$7,060 × .12 = **$847.20**

Difference from original expected commission:

$907.20 − $847.20 = **$60.00 adjustment**

## Average order value

**Total order value ÷ number of orders = AOV**

Example:

$34,200 across 6 orders:

$34,200 ÷ 6 = **$5,700 AOV**

## Reorder rate

A simple account-based reorder rate:

**Accounts that reordered ÷ accounts eligible to reorder × 100**

Example:

9 of 15 eligible accounts reordered:

9 ÷ 15 = **60%**

## Sell-through rate

**Units sold by retailer ÷ units received × 100**

Example:

Retailer received 40 units and sold 26:

26 ÷ 40 = **65% sell-through**

## Account growth

**(Current Period − Previous Period) ÷ Previous Period × 100**

Example:

Previous period purchases = $8,000  
Current period purchases = $10,400

Growth = ($10,400 − $8,000) ÷ $8,000 = **30%**

## Portfolio concentration

**Account sales ÷ total portfolio sales × 100**

Example:

Largest account = $32,000  
Portfolio total = $120,000

$32,000 ÷ $120,000 = **26.7% concentration**

The Program should teach learners to interpret this as context—not automatically “good” or “bad.”

# 6. MODULE 1 — Inside Brand Placement

**Estimated time:** 75–90 minutes

## Module purpose

Give a complete beginner a mental model of brand placement before introducing tactics, numbers, or software.

By the end of Module 1, the learner should understand:

- what brand placement means;
- how brands, representatives, buyers, and retailers relate;
- how a product moves through the commercial journey;
- common representation models;
- why placement is more than making a one-time sale;
- how Ryva organizes the same commercial relationships.

## 1.1 What Brand Placement Is

**Format:** 5–7 minute video + written field notes

### Learning objectives

The learner can:

- define brand placement in plain language;
- distinguish brand placement from direct-to-consumer selling;
- explain why retail fit matters;
- describe placement as a relationship between product, brand, retailer, and buyer.

### Core lesson

Brand placement is the commercial process of getting a brand's products into appropriate retail environments and developing the relationships that allow those products to remain commercially relevant there.

A brand can sell directly to consumers through its own site or store. Brand placement introduces another relationship: the brand sells products to a retailer, and the retailer then offers those products to its own customer.

This changes what matters.

A retailer is not simply deciding whether a product is attractive. The retailer may also consider:

- whether the product fits the store;
- whether the price architecture makes sense;
- whether customers are likely to understand it;
- how it works with the existing assortment;
- how much inventory must be purchased;
- whether there is enough margin;
- when it can ship;
- whether the brand can support reorders;
- how the relationship may develop.

Brand placement therefore sits between **brand storytelling** and **commercial decision-making**.

### In context

A $68 candle may look beautiful online.

Northline Home may see it as a natural fit because its customer expects premium decorative objects.

Juniper & Finch may like the product but determine that its typical customer prefers gifts below $50.

The product did not change. The placement context did.

### Knowledge Check 1.1

1. **Which best describes brand placement?**  
   A. Advertising a product directly to consumers  
   B. Connecting products with appropriate retail environments and developing the commercial relationship  
   C. Manufacturing products for a retailer  
   D. Setting a brand's social-media strategy  
   **Answer:** B

2. **Why can the same product make sense for one retailer and not another?**  
   **Answer:** Retailers differ in customer, assortment, price architecture, category needs, inventory strategy, and commercial priorities.

3. **True or false: if a product is attractive, it is automatically a strong retail placement.**  
   **Answer:** False. Visual appeal is only one part of commercial fit.

## 1.2 The People in the Relationship

**Format:** 5-minute video + relationship diagram

### Core participants

**Brand** — Creates and owns the product and commercial identity.

**Representative** — May help a brand build relationships with retailers, communicate product information, identify opportunities, support ordering, and maintain account continuity.

**Buyer** — Evaluates products for a retailer and makes or influences assortment decisions.

**Retailer / Account** — Purchases products for resale to its customers.

**Consumer** — Ultimately purchases the product from the retailer.

### Core idea

Everyone is looking at the same product from a different perspective.

A brand may ask:

> Does this placement support how we want the brand to grow?

A representative may ask:

> Is there a credible reason this brand and this account belong together?

A buyer may ask:

> Does this deserve space, inventory dollars, and attention in my assortment?

A retailer may ask:

> Can this product perform with our customer?

### Knowledge Check 1.2

1. Who typically evaluates whether a product belongs in a retailer's assortment?  
   **Answer:** A buyer or other merchant responsible for the category.

2. What is one reason a representative may decline to pursue an account even if the retailer is interested?  
   **Answer:** The placement may conflict with brand positioning, territory strategy, channel strategy, exclusivity, pricing, or long-term commercial fit.

3. Match the concern to the participant:  
   Brand → long-term positioning  
   Buyer → assortment fit  
   Retailer → customer performance  
   Representative → relationship and placement strategy

## 1.3 How a Product Moves

**Format:** Animated commercial journey + field notes

### Journey

> Brand → Product → Prospect → Buyer → Placement → Opening Order → Fulfillment → Account → Reorder → Ongoing Relationship

Not every opportunity reaches every step.

A prospect may decline. A buyer may request more information. A buyer may like the brand but wait for another season. An opening order may perform well and reorder. An opening order may underperform.

The point is not that the commercial journey is perfectly linear. The point is that every action belongs to a larger relationship.

### Knowledge Check 1.3

1. Does buyer interest automatically equal a placement?  
   **Answer:** No.

2. Does an opening order automatically mean an account is commercially healthy?  
   **Answer:** No. Reorders, sell-through, communication, payment, and ongoing fit add important context.

3. Put these in a plausible sequence:  
   **Buyer conversation → opening order → fulfillment → reorder**

## 1.4 Ways Brands Are Represented

**Format:** Written comparison + 4-minute overview video

### Independent representative

May represent one or more non-competing brands within a category, geography, or account group.

### Agency or showroom

A business representing multiple brands with shared sales infrastructure or market presence.

### In-house team

Employees working directly for one brand.

### Distributor

Purchases or controls inventory and resells it through its own commercial relationships, depending on the model.

### Important nuance

There is no single universal representation structure. Agreements, territories, compensation, responsibilities, and account ownership vary.

The Ryva Program teaches recurring commercial concepts—not one universal employment or representation model.

### Knowledge Check 1.4

1. Which representation model involves employees working directly for a brand?  
   **Answer:** In-house team.

2. True or false: every independent representative uses the same commission structure.  
   **Answer:** False.

3. Why is it useful to understand multiple representation models?  
   **Answer:** Because responsibilities, account relationships, compensation, territory, and commercial processes can vary.

## 1.5 Placement Is a Relationship, Not a Transaction

**Format:** Case comparison

### Case A

A retailer places a $9,000 opening order but never reorders.

### Case B

A retailer places a $3,800 opening order, reorders $2,700 three months later, then adds two additional SKUs the following season.

Which is the “better” account?

The answer cannot be determined from opening-order size alone.

Case A produced a larger first transaction.

Case B provides evidence of continued demand and account development.

The lesson is to avoid using one number as the entire commercial story.

### Knowledge Check 1.5

1. Why can a smaller opening order still become an important account?  
   **Answer:** It may lead to strong sell-through, reorders, assortment expansion, and a durable relationship.

2. What additional information would help evaluate Case A?  
   **Answer:** Sell-through, customer response, return/cancellation history, timing, inventory remaining, communication, and why no reorder occurred.

3. What does a reorder provide evidence of?  
   **Answer:** Continued retailer demand or confidence, although its meaning still depends on context.

2. Why does target customer matter to placement?

- 2 scarves;
- 1 weekender.

That is not ten unrelated products; it is an assortment with category, price, and variant structure.

### Knowledge Check 2.2

1. What is a SKU?  
   **Answer:** A distinct sellable product variation represented as its own stock-keeping unit.

2. Why might a buyer choose only five SKUs from a 30-SKU line?

3. What is a hero product?

2. Why might a buyer decline a product they genuinely like?

3. True or false: buyer “no” always means permanent lack of interest.

## 2.5 Pricing Architecture: Wholesale Price, Retail Price, Markup & Margin

**Format:** Interactive math lesson

### Example A

Alder & Vale candle:

Wholesale price: **$34**  
MSRP: **$68**

Retail markup:

($68 − $34) ÷ $34 = 100%

Retail gross margin:

($68 − $34) ÷ $68 = 50%

Gross profit dollars per unit before other retailer expenses:

$68 − $34 = $34

### Important nuance

A retailer's actual profitability depends on far more than simple product gross margin, including freight, markdowns, labor, rent, payment processing, damaged goods, returns, and operating expenses.

The Program teaches product-level commercial math, not retailer accounting.

### Knowledge Check 2.5

1. A product is purchased by the retailer for $25 and sold for $50. What is the markup?  
   **Answer:** 100%.

2. What is the gross margin?  
   **Answer:** 50%.

3. A product costs the retailer $42 and retails for $84. What are gross profit dollars per unit?  
   **Answer:** $42.

4. Why are markup and margin different?  
   **Answer:** Markup compares profit dollars to cost; margin compares profit dollars to selling price.

## 2.6 Minimums, Case Packs & Order Feasibility

Teach:

### Opening order minimum

Minimum monetary value or quantity required for an opening purchase.

### MOQ

Minimum order quantity for a product or order.

### Case pack

Required ordering multiple.

### Example

Paws & Pine ceramic bowl:

Wholesale: $24  
Case pack: 4  
Buyer wants 6.

If case-pack rules are strict, six is not an orderable quantity. The buyer may need 4 or 8.

Eight units × $24 = **$192**

### Knowledge Check 2.6

1. A product wholesales for $18 with a case pack of 6. What is the line value of one case?  
   **Answer:** $108.

2. An opening order minimum is $1,500. A buyer's cart totals $1,420. Has the minimum been met?  
   **Answer:** No.

3. Why do minimums matter when evaluating account fit?  
   **Answer:** A retailer may like the line but may not have the budget, space, or demand to support the required opening commitment.

|---|---:|

2. What is the purpose of a fit matrix?

## 3.6 Retailer Economics in Context

Connect product margin with inventory commitment.

Example:

Retailer buys 24 units at $30 wholesale.

Inventory investment:

24 × $30 = **$720**

MSRP = $60.

Potential retail sales if all units sell at full price:

24 × $60 = **$1,440**

Potential product gross profit dollars before operating expenses:

$1,440 − $720 = **$720**

Ask what happens if 8 units must be marked down.

The point is not to calculate full retailer profitability. It is to understand why buyers care about inventory productivity.

### Knowledge Check 3.6

1. 20 units at $28 wholesale require what inventory investment?  
   **Answer:** $560.

2. If MSRP is $56 and all 20 sell at full price, what are gross retail sales?  
   **Answer:** $1,120.

3. Why might a retailer prefer a smaller opening order?  
   **Answer:** To reduce inventory risk while testing customer demand.

## 4.6 Simple Pipeline Math

Example:

20 contacted

- restock;
- seasonal relevance;
- buyer request;
- prior conversation.

Which is stronger: generic persistence or relevant commercial context?

**Answer:** Relevant commercial context.

## 5.2 The Introduction Email

Teach a simple structure:

1. Why this retailer
2. What the brand is
3. What may be relevant
4. Clear next action
5. Easy access to supporting material

Best,  
[Name]

- requested material;
- approaching market appointment;

- seasonal relevance;
- line update.

- daily chasing;
- invented urgency;
- guilt;
- pretending prior contact occurred;
- misleading scarcity.

What is better than guessing when a buyer asks for information you do not have?

# 12. MODULE 7 — Account Development, Sell-Through & Commercial Performance

**Estimated time:** 95–110 minutes

## 7.1 What Happens After Placement

Teach that the opening order is the beginning of account history.

Relevant later questions:

- Did inventory arrive?
- Did product launch?
- What sold?
- What did not?
- Was there a reorder?
- Did assortment expand?
- Did communication continue?
- Is the retailer still a good fit?

### Knowledge Check 7.1

Why is “order placed” insufficient for evaluating the relationship?

**Answer:** It does not show fulfillment, sell-through, reorder behavior, payment context, assortment development, or ongoing fit.

## 7.2 Sell-Through as Context

Teach the formula again and interpretation.

Example:

Product A: 80% sell-through  
Product B: 35% sell-through

Ask: Should Product B automatically be removed?

Not necessarily.

Consider:

- how long it has been on floor;
- inventory quantity;
- season;
- store placement;
- price;
- markdown;
- local customer;
- product availability.

### Knowledge Check 7.2

Why should sell-through always be paired with time period?

**Answer:** Selling 50% in two weeks means something different from selling 50% in twelve months.

## 7.3 Reorder Rate

Example:

20 active accounts have reached a reasonable reorder window.

12 reordered.

12 ÷ 20 = **60% reorder rate**

Teach denominator discipline. Do not count accounts that have not had a realistic opportunity to reorder.

### Knowledge Check 7.3

8 of 10 eligible accounts reorder. Rate?

**Answer:** 80%.

## 7.4 Average Order Value

Orders:

$2,500  
$4,000  
$3,500  
$6,000

Total = $16,000  
AOV = $4,000

Teach that AOV can rise for good or bad reasons. One unusually large order can distort it.

### Knowledge Check 7.4

Five orders total $27,500. AOV?

**Answer:** $5,500.

## 7.5 Account Growth

Account purchases:

Prior period: $12,000  
Current period: $15,600

Growth:

($15,600 − $12,000) ÷ $12,000 = **30%**

Discuss negative growth without labeling it automatically unhealthy.

### Knowledge Check 7.5

Prior: $20,000  
Current: $17,000

Growth:

($17,000 − $20,000) ÷ $20,000 = **−15%**

## 7.6 Portfolio Concentration

Teach the risk of reading aggregate sales without knowing where they came from.

Example:

Total portfolio purchases: $150,000  
Largest account: $60,000

Concentration = 40%

Ask what would happen if that one account disappeared.

Do not tell learners a universal “safe” percentage.

### Knowledge Check 7.6

Largest account $25,000 out of $100,000. Concentration?

**Answer:** 25%.

## 7.7 Account Health

Build an educational account-health view from multiple signals:

- recent order;
- reorder pattern;
- sell-through if available;
- communication;
- payment/operational issues where known;
- assortment breadth;
- trend;
- unresolved problems.

Teach that health is a reasoned interpretation, not one universal score.

## 7.8 Ryva Workspace Preview — Accounts, Analytics & Reports

Show:

The relationship record.

### Analytics

Patterns across representatives, products, brands, buyers, pipeline, commercial activity, and portfolio.

### Reports

Exportable views for structured analysis.

Teach the distinction:

> A dashboard tells you what happened. Commercial reasoning asks what it might mean.

Expected commission:

**$1,150**

### 11. Adjustment

Expected commission was based on $9,000 at 12%.

$1,000 becomes non-commissionable.

New expected commission:

$8,000 × .12 = **$960**

### 12. Account interpretation

Account A: $8,000 opening order, no reorder after expected selling period.  
Account B: $3,500 opening order, $2,800 reorder.

Which conclusion is strongest?

A. Account A is definitely better  
B. Account B provides stronger evidence of continued purchasing, but more context is still needed  
C. Account B is definitely more profitable  
D. Opening-order value tells the full story

**Answer:** B

### 13. Ryva structure

Where does a potential brand-to-retailer opportunity belong?

**Answer:** Placements.

### 14. Ryva structure

Where should a subsequent purchase by an existing retail account be represented?

**Answer:** Reorders.

### 15. Documentation

Why preserve original order/document records?

**Answer:** To retain supporting commercial evidence and reduce ambiguity around what information was originally received.

# 17. Knowledge Check Technical Rules

Every substantive section should end with a short knowledge check.

Recommended:

- 2–4 questions;
- no score required to progress;
- immediate educational feedback;
- unlimited retry;
- randomized ordering when useful.

Knowledge checks should test understanding, interpretation, simple calculation, and the ability to distinguish concepts.

They should not exist merely to make the course feel longer.

---

# 18. Guided Practice Technical Rules

Guided Practice activities should:

- use fictional commercial contexts;
- save learner responses;
- provide “Things to notice”;
- explain tradeoffs;
- allow more than one reasonable response where appropriate;
- distinguish facts from assumptions;
- avoid professional-competency claims.

Use language such as:

- “What would you pay attention to?”
- “Which option appears strongest from the information available?”
- “What information is missing?”
- “What tradeoff do you see?”
- “Compare your reasoning.”

Avoid:

- “The professional answer is...”
- “A qualified representative would...”
- “You passed the skill...”
- “Job-ready competency...”

# 26. Content Quality Standard

Before publishing any lesson, verify:

- Does it teach a real commercial concept?
- Is the language understandable to a complete beginner?
- Does it avoid unnecessary jargon?
- If jargon is necessary, is it explained?
- Is there a believable example?
- Does the knowledge check test understanding rather than recall alone?
- Does the learner have to interpret anything?
- Is there an opportunity for practice?
- Is the math accurate?
- Does the lesson distinguish facts from assumptions?
- Does the content avoid implying professional certification or guaranteed outcomes?
- Does it use **brand placement** as the Program category?
- Are legitimate industry terms such as **wholesale price**, **wholesale account**, or **wholesale order** used only where they are the technically correct term?
- Does the Ryva connection make the eventual operating-platform unlock easier to understand?

If the answer to most of these is no, the lesson probably does not add enough value to remain in the Program.

# 27. Final Principle

Ryva should not teach brand placement as a collection of scripts.

It should teach learners how the pieces relate.

A product does not exist separately from its price.

A price does not exist separately from retailer economics.

A retailer does not exist separately from its customer.

An order does not exist separately from the account.

A commission does not exist separately from the underlying commercial activity.

And a placement does not end when the first purchase is made.

The strongest recurring question throughout The Ryva Program should remain:

> **What would you pay attention to next?**
